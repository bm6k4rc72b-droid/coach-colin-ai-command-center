Baseline

A camera pulse scan and a coach that reads it against your history — not a population chart.

  • Forty seconds of your face gives resting pulse, heart-rate variability and breathing rate, from blood flow the camera can see and you cannot.
  • The coach prescribes today's session from that reading and four questions, with the heart rates to hold and the reason it chose them.
  • Nothing leaves the device. No account, no upload, no server. Works on iPhone, Android and a laptop webcam.

The camera opens only when you tap, and frames are analysed and thrown away — never stored, never sent.

      How it works

      Seeing a pulse with a camera

      Every heartbeat pushes blood into the capillaries just under your skin, and haemoglobin absorbs green light. Your face therefore darkens and lightens by roughly half a per cent, sixty-odd times a minute. That is far too small to see and quite large enough to measure: the app averages the skin inside the oval down to one red, one green and one blue number per frame, and looks for a 0.6–3 Hz oscillation in the result.

      The colour mixing is POS (plane-orthogonal-to-skin), with CHROM as a second opinion. Both project the three channels onto combinations where anything that changes all three together — you leaning toward the lamp, the auto-exposure stepping, a cloud — cancels exactly, while the pulse survives because blood absorbs the three bands differently. That is why a small fidget does not become a heart rate.

      Why it refuses to score you at first

      A resting pulse of 58 is unremarkable in one person and a warning in another; an HRV of 30 ms is poor at twenty and excellent at sixty. Population norms cannot tell you anything useful about a single morning. So the app collects four resting scans before it will produce a readiness score at all, and from then on every number is quoted against your median and your own spread — robust statistics, so one scan taken after running up the stairs cannot redefine normal.

      What the coach is

      A decision engine, not a chat model. Readiness, your answers about sleep and soreness, and how many hard days you have strung together choose an intensity tier; the tier and your goal choose a session; your measured resting rate and age set the heart-rate ceilings by the Karvonen method. Every prescription lists the rule that produced it, and the whole thing works with the phone in aeroplane mode. If you add an API key, a language model rewords the same decision and answers follow-ups — it never overrules the rules.

      Honest limits

      • Rate is good; variability is only fair. Frequency survives a mediocre scan because it only needs the dominant oscillation. RMSSD needs the position of every individual beat, and a phone camera at 30 frames a second locates each one to a few milliseconds at best. The app withholds the HRV figure rather than print one it cannot stand behind.
      • Light and stillness are the whole game. Front-lit, indirect, no flicker, elbows supported. Backlight, a dim room, or a phone held in a moving hand will fail the quality gate, and it will tell you which.
      • It measures resting state. Scan before you get up and moving, at roughly the same time each day. A scan two minutes after exercise is a fine measurement of something, but it is not your baseline, and the app files it separately.
      • It is not a diagnosis and cannot be one. No camera app can detect illness, arrhythmia, or a heart condition. If a reading is strange for days, or you have symptoms, that is a conversation with a clinician.
      • Skin tone, make-up and facial hair all matter, because they change how much light comes back. The signal-to-noise figure on every scan is the app's honest report of how well it did on you, today.

      Privacy

      Frames are analysed in memory and discarded — nothing is recorded, and there is no server to record it to. Scans, settings and any API key live in this browser's local storage and leave only when you export them. Erasing everything in Trends erases everything.
