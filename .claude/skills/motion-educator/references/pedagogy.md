# Teaching on screen

A film that looks expensive and teaches nothing has failed. These are the rules for the explanation itself; they
apply to any subject.

## One film, one promise

Write the promise before anything else: the one thing the viewer can say in their own words afterwards ("air
scatters blue light much more than red, and that is why the sky is blue and sunsets are orange"). Every shot either
moves toward that sentence or is cut. A topic with two promises is two films.

## The order that works

1. **The question**, in the viewer's words, in the first three seconds, over a picture worth looking at.
2. **The tension**: why the obvious answer does not work ("but sunlight is white").
3. **The pieces, one at a time**, each concrete before it is abstract: show the thing, then name it, then measure it.
4. **The mechanism**: the pieces put together, as an experiment the viewer watches (red goes through, blue is
   thrown everywhere), before any formula.
5. **The number**, once, with what it is computed from and something to compare it with.
6. **The objection** the attentive viewer already has ("then why not violet?"), answered honestly.
7. **The consequence** somewhere else ("and that is also why sunsets are orange"): this is what makes it feel like
   understanding and not a fact.
8. **The recap**, in one frame, then one line to remember.

## Rules of thumb

- **One idea a shot.** If the caption has an "and", look for the cut.
- **Show the same thing twice, changed once.** Two shots that differ in exactly one thing (a red ray, then a blue
  ray, through the same molecules) teach more than a paragraph.
- **Numbers need a neighbour.** "5,9 times stronger" means something; "the coefficient is 5,86" does not. Round to
  what the viewer can hold, and keep the exact figure in the fact sheet.
- **Labels sit on the thing they name**, with a leader line when they cannot. Never a legend the eye has to travel to.
- **Colour means something and keeps meaning it.** If blue is "short wavelength" in shot 7 it is not "selected" in
  shot 12. The accent colour is for the point of the film.
- **Captions are short sentences in plain words**, with the one or two words that carry the point in the accent.
  About 17 characters a second is comfortable to read; the storyboard check holds you to it.
- **Scale honestly.** If a diagram exaggerates (an atmosphere drawn thick so it can be seen), say so on screen.
- **Do not animate what does not explain.** Decoration competes with the point. The light, the camera and the
  motion should each be doing a teaching job: directing the eye, connecting two ideas, or showing a change.
- **Leave out what is true but not needed.** Note the simplification in the fact sheet; do not put it on screen
  unless leaving it out would mislead.

## Truth

- Every claim and every number is in the fact sheet with its source before it is storyboarded.
- Prefer primary and institutional sources (the original paper, a standards body, a space agency, a textbook).
- Do the arithmetic yourself and keep it in the claim (`(700/450)^4 = 5,86`).
- When sources disagree or the honest answer is "it depends", say the version that is true for the case the film
  shows, and say which case that is.
- Never invent a quote, a statistic, a date or an attribution. If you could not check something, tell the person in
  the delivery note which facts came from your own knowledge and were not looked up.
