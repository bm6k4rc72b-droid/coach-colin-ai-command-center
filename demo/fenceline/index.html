    The method

    It watches the herd, not the predator

    A coyote is small, fast, and usually in the brush. Twenty-four cattle reacting to it are large, slow, and lit up across the whole frame. So the detector's job is not to find the predator — it's to notice that the herd stopped behaving like a herd that is grazing.

    SIGNAL 1

    Bunching

    Mean nearest-neighbour distance collapses. Grazing cattle spread out to eat; frightened cattle pull in tight. This is the earliest and most reliable of the four, and it's measurable from a top-down track set without any species classifier at all.

    SIGNAL 2

    Polarisation

    Headings align. Grazing herds point every direction; a herd under threat points one way — away. The alignment vector, reversed, is where the threat is, which is how the system calls a bearing before anything appears on camera.

    SIGNAL 3

    Heads up, then speed

    Head elevation rises across the group within a couple of seconds — a social cascade, not individual startle. Speed follows last. By the time speed moves, you're already late, which is exactly why speed alone makes a bad alarm.

    The dwell timer is the product

    Any of these will spike on a passing truck, a deer, a hand walking the line, or a cow that startles herself. The index has to hold above threshold for a sustained window before it fires — that bar filling under the number in the console is the false-positive control, and it's the single setting that decides whether a rancher keeps the app on their phone or mutes it in week two.

    Every alert carries its own evidence

    An alert that says "something happened" gets ignored. This one arrives with the clip, the bearing, the head count, and which four signals moved — so the person waking up at 2 a.m. can decide from bed whether to pull boots on, and can prove afterward what took the calf.

    Beyond the video

    What would make it worth paying for

    Predator detection alone is a feature, not a business — most nights nothing happens, and a product that is silent for eleven months gets forgotten. The same cameras and the same tracker earn their keep every single day if you point them at these too.

    Acoustic triangulation

    NEEDS FIELD DATA

    Three solar mic posts and time-difference-of-arrival puts a coyote chorus in a pasture quadrant. Audio is 360°, works through brush and fog where a camera is blind, costs a fraction of a camera, and coyotes announce themselves.

    The rooster as a sensor

    IN DEMO

    Roosters give different alarm calls for aerial and ground threats, and they are faster than any camera. Classify the call, and the flock's own warning system becomes the trigger that tells the vision model where to look. Switch the console to Poultry · Dawn to watch it fire.

    Calving watch

    NEEDS FIELD DATA

    Same tracker, different pattern: isolation from the group, restlessness, tail elevation, up-down cycling. Predicts calving hours out and — the part with real money in it — flags a stalled labour so someone gets there in time.

    Head count reconciliation

    IN DEMO

    Count at the water, count at the gate, count at dusk. "47 of 49 crossed to the north pasture" is a whole class of problems caught early — predation, sickness, a downed fence, theft — and it needs no behaviour model at all.

    Off-feed and off-water flags

    NEEDS FIELD DATA

    Per-animal trough visits. An animal that stops drinking is often sick a day or two before it looks sick to a person on horseback. This is the feature that runs on the 364 nights nothing tries to eat your calves.

    Automated deterrent

    IN DEMO

    Strobe, siren, and a recorded human voice on the post nearest the bearing — response inside two seconds instead of twenty minutes. Press Deter in the console during an alert and watch the index fall. Rotate the sound so nothing habituates.

    Guardian dog tracking

    NEEDS FIELD DATA

    The livestock guardian dog is the actual defence system. Track whether it's with the herd, whether it left, and whether it's alerting — a dog moving hard toward the fence line is itself one of the best predictors available.

    Depredation evidence pack

    NEEDS FIELD DATA

    Confirmed-kill compensation programmes exist; the barrier is proof. Auto-assemble the clip, timestamp, GPS, and species call into the format a claim needs. That converts an alert into a cheque, which is the easiest ROI conversation there is.

    Season hotspot map

    Every incident is a pin with a time. By the second season you know which draw and which two hours of night to worry about — so the dog, the fladry, and the calving pasture all move to where the data says.

    Run of show

    The eight-minute demo

    Built to run on a laptop on a truck hood with no internet. Nothing here needs a live camera, an account, or a signal — which is also the point you are making about the product.

    1. Open on boredom

      Console running, index sitting at four, nothing happening. Say: "this is a normal night, and this is what it looks like for eleven months." Let it sit for a beat longer than is comfortable. The calm baseline is what makes the spike mean something.

      ~40 SEC · SAY NOTHING FOR THE LAST TEN

    2. Hand them the mouse

      Let the rancher press Release predator themselves. Do not narrate over it. Watch where their eyes go — most people watch the cattle, not the numbers, which is the right instinct and worth telling them so.

      ~60 SEC

    3. Point at the bearing before the predator is visible

      The console calls the direction while the coyote is still in the draw. This is the moment the whole idea lands: the herd told the system where the threat was. "Your cattle already knew. Nobody was awake to hear them."

      THE HINGE OF THE DEMO

    4. Let the phone buzz

      The alert card slides in with the bearing, the count, and the clip. Ask directly: at 2 a.m., is that enough to get you out of bed, or too little, or too much? Their answer is your product spec, so write it down in front of them.

      ~90 SEC · ASK, THEN STOP TALKING

    5. Press Deter and show the index fall

      Detection is interesting; response is what they're buying. Sound the post, watch the predator break off and the herd settle back to baseline over about a minute — and note that this happened without anyone waking up.

      ~60 SEC

    6. Switch to the coop

      Poultry · Dawn. The rooster calls before the vision model has anything, and the door closes on its own. If they keep birds, this is the part they'll repeat to somebody else later — and it demonstrates the same engine on a completely different animal.

      ~70 SEC · THE ONE THEY RETELL

    7. Type their numbers into the calculator

      Their head count, their losses, their guess at what a 90-second warning saves. Let them argue the percentage down — it's more persuasive when they set it, and a deal that only works at an optimistic number isn't a deal.

      ~2 MIN · THEY DRIVE

    8. Say plainly what it can't do

      It is not a fence, it will wake you for deer, and it needs a season of footage from their ground before the thresholds are right. Volunteering the limits is what separates this from every other pitch they've sat through — and the honest ask at the end is for one camera on one paddock for one season, not a purchase order.

      ~60 SEC · CLOSE HERE
    Straight answers

    What this demo is and isn't

    • The scene is a simulation, and it's labelled as one on screen. Cattle and birds are agents in a flocking model with a fear-contagion term; the predator is another agent. Nothing here is footage of a real ranch and it must never be shown as if it were.
    • The telemetry is computed, not scripted. The risk index, bearing, dwell timer and fence estimate are all derived from the actual positions and velocities on screen each frame — the same four measurements a real pipeline would take from a tracker. Change the outcome by pressing Deter and every number responds. That's why it holds up when a rancher pokes at it.
    • What a real build still needs. A detector and multi-object tracker tuned to livestock at distance and at night, a season of labelled footage from the actual property, thermal or good IR for the hours that matter, and threshold calibration per pasture. The behaviour maths above is the easy part; the data is the whole job.
    • The hard problem is false alarms, not detection. Deer, wind, weather, a barn cat, a hand checking the line. An alarm that cries wolf twice a week is worse than no alarm, because it trains the one person who matters to ignore it.
    • It is not a fence and not a guardian dog. It shortens the time between something starting and somebody knowing. Sell that, and nothing more than that.
