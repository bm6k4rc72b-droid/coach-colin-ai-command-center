The honest status

The laptop demo is not a system.
Here is what closing that gap costs.

What exists today runs a real tracker on real pixels and proves the idea in a meeting. It is not something a rancher can rely on overnight, and pretending otherwise is how this kind of project loses the only customer it had. The gap is mostly not code — it is hardware, power, connectivity, and a season of footage nobody has collected yet.

What the browser version is genuinely good for

  • Showing a ranch owner the idea in eight minutes, offline, with no install and no account.
  • Scrubbing a recorded trail-cam clip — drag the file in and get the timestamps where anything moved, instead of watching four hours of empty field.
  • Watching a calving pen overnight from a laptop on a bucket: zone watch, alarm sound on, thumbnail at each trigger. Narrow, but real, and free.
  • Proving the alarm logic to yourself: drag the dwell slider to zero and watch the alarm become useless.
  • A development bench — every stage is visible, so you can see which one is failing.

Why it is not operational

  • It detects motion, not livestock. No species classifier, so a truck and a cow are the same thing to it.
  • It runs only while a browser tab is open and visible. A screen wake lock keeps the machine awake, but background the tab and the watch stops.
  • The alarm is a beep on that laptop, in that room, plus three thumbnails. Nothing reaches a phone, nothing is recorded, nobody else is told.
  • No night vision, no weather sealing, no power at the post, no clip retention, no camera-offline check.
  • Every threshold is hand-set. A real install learns them from that camera, on that fence line.
The route

Four phases, and what you are actually waiting for

The timeline is not set by how fast anyone writes code. It is set by how long it takes for enough real nights to happen on that property — including the ones with an incident, which are rare on purpose. Plan around that and the project is calm; ignore it and you will ship a detector tuned on somebody else's ranch.

PHASE 0WEEKS 0–8NO AI AT ALL

Put cameras up and record

Pick the one paddock where the losses actually are — usually the calving pasture or whichever draw the coyotes work. Two to four cameras, recording continuously, keeping every night. This phase builds nothing clever and it is the phase most projects skip, which is why they fail in month five.

Continuous video matters more than resolution here. Herd behaviour is a time series; a PIR-triggered trail cam that fires a three-second burst gives you evidence photos but not the behaviour signal. If budget forces a choice, fewer continuous cameras beat more triggered ones.

Gate to the next phaseSeveral weeks of night footage in hand, including at least one incident the rancher can point at and say "that one — that is what I want to know about."
PHASE 1WEEKS 4–18OVERLAPS PHASE 0

Label the footage and train the detector

Sample diversely rather than densely — consecutive frames are near-duplicates and teach the model almost nothing. Aim for a few thousand labelled frames spread across day and night, near and far, standing and lying and running, rain and fog and hard sun, and every season you have.

The shortcut worth taking: run an off-the-shelf COCO-pretrained YOLO over the footage first. cow, horse, sheep, bird and dog are already COCO classes, so it will pre-label most of your frames and a person only corrects them. That turns weeks of labelling into days.

Then fine-tune YOLO or RT-DETR from those weights. A few hours on a rented GPU is genuinely all the training compute this needs — this is not a problem that wants a cluster.

Gate to the next phaseOn held-out nights from this property: recall on the incidents you care about, and false alerts per camera per week under one. Not mAP, and not accuracy.
PHASE 2WEEKS 14–28THE FIRST REAL INSTALL

Move it onto the post

Edge compute in a sealed box, camera on a mast well above what cattle can reach, power that survives December, and an alert path that works when the network does not. This is the phase where the software stops being the hard part and mounting hardware in weather becomes the hard part.

Design rule for the whole build: the local response must not depend on the network. The siren, the strobe and the coop door fire from the box on the post. The phone notification is a bonus that arrives when there is a link.

Gate to the next phaseThirty consecutive nights unattended with no manual intervention, no missed camera-offline event, and the rancher's own verdict that the alerts were worth waking up for.
PHASE 3MONTH 7 ONWARDA SEASON, MINIMUM

Make it a system rather than a camera

More paddocks, per-camera learned thresholds, the dismissal loop feeding retraining, and the features that earn their keep on the 364 nights nothing tries to eat a calf: calving watch, head-count reconciliation, off-water flags, the season hotspot map.

You need a full year before you know what normal looks like. Vegetation grows and dies, the sun angle swings, calving changes the herd's behaviour completely, and a threshold tuned in August will be wrong in January.

The thing people size wrong

Power budget for a solar post

Almost every failed off-grid install failed in December. Size the array for your worst month of sun, not your average, and derate for a panel that is dusty, cold, badly angled and behind a charge controller that is not perfect. Put your own numbers in.

Jetson Orin Nano ~7–15 W under load. Pi 5 + Hailo ~8 W.
More with IR illuminators running all night.
LTE modem or WiFi bridge. LoRa is negligible.
December, at your latitude. Not the annual average.
How many overcast days in a row it must survive.
Dust, angle, temperature, controller losses.

Duty-cycling is the cheapest watt you will ever save. A detector that runs at 5 fps between dusk and dawn and idles by day cuts the compute draw by more than half, and daylight predation is not the problem you are solving.

At the house, Starlink or whatever fixed link exists carries the clips and the app. The alert path and the local response must both survive that link being down, because it will be down exactly when the weather is bad, which is exactly when livestock get into trouble.

Field reality

What actually breaks

None of these are software problems, and all of them will happen before month six. They are listed roughly in order of how often they take a camera out.

  • Spiders. Webs across the lens, every warm night, drawn by the IR illuminator. This is the number one cause of a camera going useless in the field and it is almost never in anyone's plan. Budget a physical cleaning schedule, and detect it in software — a frame whose sharpness collapses is a maintenance alert, not a predator.
  • Condensation and frost. Sealed enclosures still breathe through temperature swings. Desiccant packs, a vent with a membrane, and a lens hood.
  • Cattle. They rub on anything at rubbing height, and they will take out a mast that a person thought was solid. Mount high, brace properly, and assume it gets tested.
  • Rodents. They eat cable insulation, particularly in a warm enclosure in winter. Conduit everything.
  • Batteries in the cold. LiFePO4 must not be charged below freezing without a heater or a low-temperature cutoff — a detail that quietly kills a battery bank over one winter.
  • Sun angle. A camera pointed anywhere near where the sun will be at some hour of some month gets a washed-out frame and a burst of false detections. Walk the site at the times that matter, not just at noon in summer.
  • The human end. An alert with nobody to answer it is worth nothing. Decide before installation who gets woken, and what they are supposed to do at 2 a.m. That conversation is part of the deployment, not an afterthought.
Division of labour

What I can build, and what needs boots on the ground

Software I can write end to end

The edge service — capture, inference, tracker, behaviour signals, alarm gating, deterrent relay, clip ring-buffer, health heartbeat, watchdog and remote update. The training pipeline, from the COCO pre-label bootstrap through fine-tuning to the evaluation harness that reports false alerts per camera-week. The alert path — push, SMS fallback, LoRa message format. The phone and web app, the review-and-dismiss loop that feeds retraining, the head-count and calving logic, and the evidence-pack export. Plus the install runbook and the diagnostics a rancher can run themselves.

What no amount of code substitutes for

Somebody has to walk the property and choose the camera positions, dig the cable, set the masts, aim the lenses, and come back when a spider has a different opinion. Somebody has to run Phase 0 long enough that the footage exists — that is calendar time, and it cannot be bought or parallelised. Somebody has to sit with the rancher and decide what an alert means at 2 a.m. And crucially: the dataset. A model trained on someone else's pasture is a guess dressed up as a product.

If you want the shortest honest path to something real: Tier A on one paddock, recording tonight. Everything else in this document is downstream of having the footage, and nothing downstream can start until it exists.
