ON-DEVICE
    The pipeline

    Eight stages, and what each becomes in production

    Everything in the left column is running right now, on your device, at the frame rate shown in the header. The right column is what each stage is replaced by once there is a labelled dataset and a box on the post — the interfaces between stages do not change, which is the point of building it this way.

    01

    Decode & downscale

    Frames land on a 256-wide working buffer, greyscaled. Detection does not need pixels; only the drawing does. This one decision is most of the reason it runs on a phone.

    PRODUCTIONHardware decode straight to a GPU tensor. Full res retained for the clip, not for inference.
    02

    Illumination compensation

    Gain-match the frame to the background model before differencing, and flag any global step. A cloud crossing the sun moves every pixel at once, which is otherwise a whole-frame false detection.

    PRODUCTIONUnchanged. A learned detector still needs this in front of the motion gate.
    03

    Background model

    Per-pixel running average with a slow time constant, and a noise σ estimated from the difference histogram each frame — the threshold is a multiple of that σ, not a fixed number, so it survives grain at night. Foreground pixels update more slowly, or a standing animal dissolves; but any pixel foreground for more than two and a half seconds is absorbed outright, or the model scars and every path anything ever walked stays foreground until the blobs merge into one growing mass.

    PRODUCTIONMOG2 or a small temporal net. Its job shrinks to gating: decide which frames deserve the detector.
    04

    Morphology

    Erode once to kill speckle, dilate twice to close an animal that broke into pieces. Grass moving in wind is high-frequency and dies at the erode; a cow does not.

    PRODUCTIONDropped. A detector outputs boxes directly, with no mask to clean up.
    05

    Connected components

    Flood fill over the mask into blobs with a bounding box, area and centroid. This is the stage that produces detections — the thing the tracker consumes.

    PRODUCTIONSwap in YOLO or RT-DETR fine-tuned on livestock. Same output shape: boxes plus scores.
    06

    Detection filter

    Reject on area, aspect ratio and fill ratio, and count every rejection by reason — the panel on the right is that counter. A swaying branch fails fill; a bug on the lens fails area; a shadow edge fails aspect. Then merge boxes that all but touch, because a moving animal arrives as two detections — itself, and the ghost of where it just was.

    PRODUCTIONBecomes a confidence threshold plus class gating, and the same per-reason counters stay.
    07

    Multi-object tracker

    SORT: predict every track forward with a constant-velocity Kalman filter, match to this frame's detections greedily inside a distance gate, then birth, update or kill. A track earns an ID after three hits and a quarter second, and survives a full second of misses before it dies — lifetimes in seconds, not frames, or the tracker behaves differently on every device.

    PRODUCTIONByteTrack or OC-SORT, plus an appearance embedding so an animal keeps its ID through an occlusion.
    08

    Behaviour & alarm

    Nearest-neighbour spacing, heading polarisation and speed over the confirmed tracks, against a rolling baseline — then the dwell timer and the re-alert lockout decide whether a phone buzzes.

    PRODUCTIONSame maths, plus head-pose keypoints and per-pasture thresholds learned from that property's own footage.
    Today, with no hardware

    Three things a rancher can genuinely do with this now

    Not a watch system — that needs the hardware in the deployment plan. But the page is not a toy either, and these three uses are real work that a spare laptop and ten minutes will do. Switch the mode control to Zone watch for the first two: the herd statistic needs a group, while a gate or a pen just needs "did something come in here and stay".

    USE 1

    Watch a calving pen

    Laptop on a bucket, camera on the pen, draw the zone around it, alarm sound on. It beeps when something moves in there and holds a thumbnail of the frame that triggered it. For the couple of nights you are already sleeping badly, that is worth having — and it costs nothing.

    USE 2

    Scrub a trail-cam card

    Open a video file and let the tracker run at speed. You get the timestamps where anything moved, instead of scrubbing four hours of an empty field by hand. This is the one that saves real time, today, with footage the rancher already has.

    USE 3

    Test a camera position before you mount it

    Hold a phone where the camera would go and see what the pipeline actually picks up from that angle — how much of the paddock, what the sun does, whether the far end is too far. Twenty minutes here saves a mast in the wrong place.

    What was added to make those possible

    An audible alarm, because a silent one needs somebody already staring at the screen. A screen wake lock so the laptop does not sleep mid-watch. A thumbnail of the frame at each trigger, so you can tell a cow from a barn cat without having been there. And zone watch, because the herd statistic cannot fire on a single animal by design.

    What it still is not

    The alarm is a beep on that machine, in that room. Nothing reaches a phone, nothing is recorded beyond three thumbnails, a laptop webcam sees nothing in a dark barn without light, and the tab must stay open and visible. It detects motion, not livestock. Overnight, unattended, in weather, at range, is the hardware build — not this.

    The hard part

    Six layers between a moving pixel and a phone buzzing

    Detection is a solved problem you can buy. False alarms are the whole product, because a system that cries wolf twice a week trains the one person who matters to ignore it — and an ignored alarm is worse than no alarm, since it was also paid for.

    LAYER 1

    Reject at the blob

    Area, aspect and fill bounds thrown before anything reaches the tracker. Cheap, and it removes the overwhelming majority: insects on the lens, rain streaks, a single shimmering leaf.

    LAYER 2

    Reject at the track

    A detection must persist across three frames with plausible motion before it becomes a track. Almost nothing spurious survives this — noise does not travel in a straight line at a believable speed.

    LAYER 3

    Reject at the global frame

    If most of the frame changed at once, it was the light, the camera, or a spider walking across the lens. Suppress, relearn the background, and log it rather than alerting.

    LAYER 4

    Require a group, not an animal

    The alarm is a statistic over many tracks measured against that camera's own rolling baseline. One startled animal cannot raise it. This is what makes the herd approach robust rather than clever.

    LAYER 5

    Dwell, then lock out

    Hold above threshold for a sustained window before firing, then refuse to fire again for a cooldown. Try dragging the dwell slider to zero and moving in front of the camera — you can watch the alarm become useless.

    LAYER 6

    Learn from the dismissals

    Every alert ships with its clip and a one-tap "that was nothing". Those become the hard-negative set that retrains the thresholds for that specific camera on that specific fence line.

    The metric to hold yourself to

    Not accuracy. False alerts per camera per week, with a target below one, measured against recall on a held-out set of real incidents. Accuracy on a balanced test set is meaningless when the base rate is one event per thousand nights — a model that always says "nothing" scores 99.9%.

    Where the thresholds actually come from

    Not from a config file shipped with the product. Each camera watches its own scene for a couple of weeks and learns what normal looks like there — this paddock at this hour in this wind. A threshold tuned on someone else's ranch is a guess, and it is the single most common way systems like this fail in the field.

    Straight answers

    What this bench is not

    • It is not a species classifier. It detects motion and tracks it. It will track you, your dog, a truck and a ceiling fan with equal enthusiasm, and it is labelled that way on screen. Stage 05 is where a livestock detector drops in; nothing downstream of it changes.
    • The tracker, though, is the real thing. Constant-velocity Kalman prediction, greedy assignment inside a distance gate, three-hit confirmation and twelve-miss deletion is SORT, and SORT is what production systems actually run — modern trackers are refinements of it, not replacements.
    • Nothing leaves your device. No frame, no clip, no telemetry. There is no network call in this page at all beyond the two font files.
    • The alarm is local, and only local. It beeps on the machine running the page and holds three thumbnails. There is no push notification, no SMS, no recording, and no second person told. That is a deliberate consequence of nothing leaving the device, and it is the first thing the hardware build changes.
    • Camera access can be blocked in an embedded frame or by your own permission choice. That is not a failure to hide — the synthetic source runs the identical pipeline so you can still see the tracker and the alarm gating work.
    • A motionless animal is an invisible animal. Background subtraction sees change, so a cow that stands still for long enough is absorbed into the background and its track dies. Watch the confirmed count sag when the synthetic herd settles. No amount of tuning fixes this — it is the structural reason a real deployment puts a learned detector at stage 05, because a detector sees a cow whether or not it moved.
    • Daylight tuning is not night tuning. Everything you can adjust here would be learned per camera in a real deployment, and the numbers that work in this room will not be the numbers that work on a pasture at 2 a.m.
